(From [Bewley et al, 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1602.00763.pdf]))
>"The method by Geiger et al uses the Hungarian algorithm in a two stage process. First, tracklets are formed by associating detections across adjacent frames where both geometry and appearance cues are combined to form the affinity matrix. Then, the tracklets are associated to each other to bridge broken trajectories caused by occlusion, again using both geometry and appearance cues. This two step association method restricts this approach to batch computation. Our approach is inspired by the tracking component of [20], however we simplify the association to a single stage with basic cues as described in the next section."

Bewley and colleagues provide a barebones implementation for performing multiple object tracking. They initially premise their work by stating that the success of tracking depends heavily on the detection accuracy. Next, instead of thinking about all possible edge cases, they simply look at the detections from the previous and the current frame and associate them. The authors state that there are two ways of going about this: one could follow a two-stage process where the consecutive frames are associated independently to form tracklets and later these tracklets over several frames are considered to identify identities. This would amount to be being a batch process since you can not do an online tracking and must wait for several frames to be processed, before you associate tracklets. So, instead the authors opt for a one-stage process where tracklets and identities are identified on the go.

Fast R-CNN is the strategy used for identifying bounding box detections in each frame. Next for each actual object, they use a linear constant velocity model which as the authors say, is independent of the other objects and the camera motion. The complete state space for each object includes the scale and aspect ratio, in addition to the x, y locations of the bounding box centroids. 

The following comments from the authors are the key:

>"In assigning detections to existing targets, each target’s bounding box geometry is estimated by predicting its new location in the current frame. The assignment cost matrix is then computed as the intersection-over-union (IOU) distance between each detection and all predicted bounding boxes from the existing targets. The assignment is solved optimally using the Hungarian algorithm.  Additionally, a minimum IOU is imposed to reject assignments where the detection to target overlap is less than IOU min"

They use a unique constant velocity model which predicts the state of each object  at the next time step. This set of predicted bounding boxes are considered and associated with the actual detections in the next time step using a constrained Hungarian Algorithm. The constraint is that for an association to be considered as valid, there must be atleast a minimum overlap. Once this association is achieved, we can provide feedback to the constant velocity model for each object to refine its model estimates and this process is continued for the next time steps. 

The benefit of this approach is that the complexity of associating tracklets is drastically reduced. I suuppose that choosing IOU min is critical as it could mess up associations if wrongly chosen. One weakness perhaps of this approach is that it only looks at the geometry and position  of each detection and discards all appearance (texture) related information. 

I was wondering about how to extend this to biological detections where divisions are possible. One way of doing it could be to replace hungarian algorithm with a more general linear optimization problem where each object in the previous frame can be at the most asociated to 2 objects in the next time frame. The IOU min idea can be retained from before and it would signify a conservation of volume between the parent detection in the previous frame and its two daughter detections in the next frame. 


A second alteration suited for biological data is to discard the bounding box state space parameters for a in-circle or in-ellipsoid since biological nuclei tend to elliptical. Hence, instead of including the scale and aspect ratio, one would consider the two focal lengths of a fitted ellipse to the segmentation.
