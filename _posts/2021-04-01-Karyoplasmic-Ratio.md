(From [Malerba et al, 2021](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/pdfdirect/10.1002/evl3.243?download=true))
>"One of the oldest tenets of biology is that the ratio between the nucleus size and the cell size (the “N:C” or “karyoplasmic” ratio) is roughly constant. In this work, we challenged the tenet of a constant N:C ratio by comprehensively mapping these two fundamental traits across the Tree of Life. ... Our meta-analyses revealed a previously unrecognized systematic pattern in N:C ratios at all biological scales: Larger cells have relatively smaller nuclei across all scales of biological organization – from among species, to within-species, to among-lineages of a species that was artificially size-selected."

Malerba et al, 2021 investigate an old tenet of biology that N:C ratio is broadly constant i.e. smaller cells have smaller nuclei and vice versa. They mention the *Limiting Pool Hypothesis* which states that the size of the nucleus is defined by the amount of local resources in the surrounding cell cytoplasm. This hypothesis indicates a positive correlation between cell and nuclei size but does not explain whether the dependence of the nuclei size becomes nonlinear with respect to a larger cell size, for example.

The authors find that while larger cells have larger absolute nuclei sizes, the N:C ratio is smaller for larger nuclei. Next, the authors attempt to explain this with a metabolic justification. They say, that in eukaryotes, cellular metabolism scales hypoallometrically with cell size i.e. that larger cells tend to have lower metabolism, which also suggests that larger cells with their lower metabolic needs might be able to meet their functions with relative smaller nuclei.

However, they also state that the *causality* is unclear, and hence it is not evident whether larger cells with relative small nuclei cause a lower metabolism or whether larger cells with lower metabolism cause a relative smaller nuclei. 

In their studies and quantitative analyses, the DNA content was used as a proxy to measure the nuclei size.
