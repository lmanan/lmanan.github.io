(From [Vopalensky et al, 2019](https://bmcbiol.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12915-019-0705-x))
>"The larval brain and head sensory organs develop from 11 pairs of bilateral founders, each giving rise to identical clones on the right and left body sides. Relating the origin of each bilateral founder pair back to the spiral cleavage pattern, we uncover highly divergent origins: while some founder pairs originate from corresponding cells in the spiralian lineage on each body side, others originate from non-corresponding cells, and yet others derive from a single cell within one quadrant."

Vopalensky et al address the question of how Platynereis makes a transition from rotational to bilateral symmetry. The authors explain that the first two cleavages divide the animal-vegetal axis into four blastomeres which give rise to the A, B, C and D quadrants. The subsequent cleavages are asymmetrical giving rise to the smaller micromeres which move towards the animal pole and the bigger macromeres which move towards the vegetal pole. However, at some point in the development, the embryo attains a bilateral symmetry, which was a puzzling question until this work. The authors claim that in the hyposhere, the bilateral symmetry is obtained from the two cells *2d112* and *4d* which  divide once into the left and right bilateral founder cells, and then give rise to the trunk ectoderm and mesoderm respectively. However, in the epishere, the situation is more complicated as the bilateral symmetry has to emerge from the rotationally distributed micromeres. E. B. Wilson, way back in 1892, also observed this sudden transition from rotational to bilateral symmetry (after the appearance of the prototroch) and attributed it to a pair of bilateral founders which were (until this work) yet to be identified. 

The authors state in the introduction that the initial broad development of the blastomeres gets restricted towards cell fates because of two modes - (i) conditional development (followed by cnidarians, sea urchins etc) where all blastomeres carry a broad development potential and (ii) mosaic development (followed by spiralia), where blastomeres inherit maternal determinants. Next, the authors, similar to E.B. Wilson's hypothesis, found an extensive array of bilateral founder cells distributed in the episphere at 12 hpf. The authors map gene expression of the head patterning genes *otx* and *six3* to the developmental lineage and find that while *otx* marks bilateral founders arising from the same lineage, *six3* marks bilateral founders arising from different lineages. 

The annelid brain is formed entirely from the offspring of the micromeres 1a, 1b, 1c and 1d. In order to do live imaging and lineage tracing, the authors injected embryos at a very early stage of development (1, 2 or 4 hpf) with *h2a-rfp* and *lyn-egfp* which label the chromatin and the cell memebranes respectively, and a nuclear tracer for observing the stereotypicity of cell divisions across embryos.

Next, they linked lineages to gene expression. This was done by creating a WMISH atlas with a total of 23 genes for 7 stages. By using the computational tool ProSPR, the authors found that the transcription factors *coe*, *ngn*, *neuroD* and *prox* are co-expressed with neuronal differentiation markers *elav* and *syt*. Their work contains a host of other interesting findings which shed light on the transition from rotational to bilateral symmetry in Platynereis brain.
