(From [Rodriques et al, 2019](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aaw1219))
>"The functions of complex tissues are fundamentally tied to the organization of their resident cell types. Multiplexed *in situ* hybridization approaches can measure gene expression with subcellular spatial resolution but require specialized knowledge and equipment, as well as the upfront selection of gene sets for measurements."

Rodriques et al, 2019 motivate their work *SlideSeq* by stating that *in situ* hybridization based techniques require knowing which genes need to be sequenced and have low through-put. In [this](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mQXr7ZsojpE) well-explained talk by the first author and [this](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UwoSLWlyC74) other talk by Fei Chen, they mention that *in situ* hybridization approaches have spatial context but low content, while current single cell sequencing based approaches have high content but low spatial context. 

To address spatial transcriptomics and to bridge these two approaches mentioned above, the authors of SlideSeq pack uniquely DNA-barcoded 10 microns large beads in several arrays onto a rubber-coated glass coverslip in a single monolayer. The sequence of each bead can be determined by a technique called *SOLiD*. Next, frozen tissue sections are transferred onto the dried bead surface via cryo sectioning. mRNA is released by the tissue onto the beads.

The authors developed a computational approach called *NMFreq* which reconstructs the expression of each bead as a weighted contribution of cell type signatures (each bead is approximately the size of a cell but since one can't control where the bead lands, hence such a factorization is needed). 

The authors say that the primary cost of *SlideSeq* is the cost of short-read sequencing, which is between 200 and 500 USD for the beads. The scripts used with SlideSeq are available [here](https://github.com/broadchenf/Slideseq).
