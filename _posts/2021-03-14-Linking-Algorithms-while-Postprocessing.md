In [ELEPHANT](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2021.02.26.432552v2.full.pdf), the authors learn a flow between a pair of images. Then coupled with detections available separately, during inference, they backtrack from the last frame to the first. It appears to me that they look for the distance between the estimated position of the detection at the previous time step and the available detections at that previous time step and choose that detection for linking which is below a certain distance threshold. They then ensure that each nucleus at a previous time step can only accept two links. If there is a possibility for more than two links at a nucleus at the previous time step, they go with the candidate that offers a lower displacement, and consider the next nearest nearest neighbors for the unlinked detection(s). 

This greedy association would probably suffer when the time lapse movie which needs to be tracked during inference is undersampled in time (and so is different from the frames used for training, which may have a higher sampling rate). It would also generally suffer in case of highly dense regions on account of employing a greedy approach and not solving an optimal ILP. 

Compared to KIT-SCH-GE, where the flow is estimated using phase correlation and not learnt, `ELEPHANT` has an advantage. But since, in the latter work, the authors finally end up building the lineage tree through greedy associations, it is not clear to me if they would outperform KIT-Sch-GE on most datasets.

(From [Sugawara et al, 2021](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2021.02.26.432552v2.full.pdf))
>"These estimated coordinates are subsequently used to find the parent of the nucleus at the previous timepoint by the nearest neighbor algorithm (a similar concept was introduced for 2D phase contrast microscopy data). The pairs with a distance smaller than `dsearch`  are considered as link candidates, where the closer the Euclidean distance between the two points, the higher their priority of being the correct link. Each nucleus accepts either one or two links, determined by the estimated displacements and actual distances. Briefly, given that a single nucleus has two possible links, it can accept both if at least one of the estimated displacements is larger than the threshold `ddisp` or both distances are smaller than the threshold `ddist`.  If there are competing links beyond the allowed maximum of two links, the links with smaller `ddisp`  are adopted and the remaining nucleus looks for the next closest nucleus up to `Nmax` neighbors. The links are generated by repeating the above procedure until all the nuclei get linked or the iteration count reaches to five. We optionally implement an interpolation algorithm, in which each orphan nucleus tries to find its source up to Tmax timepoints back and is linked with a nucleus at the estimated coordiante based on the flow prediction, interpolating the points in between"

Following a similar workflow but with an ILP post processing, in Linajea by [Malin-Mayor et al, 2021](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2021.07.28.454016v1.full.pdf), the authors first extract a candidate (directed) graph by backtracking. Here initially the candidate graph is obtained by first applying the flow to the detections at the *t th* time point and then querying for each estimated detection at $t-1 th$ time point, which other actual detections at $t-1 th$ time point fall in a certain window around it. The idea is that this would generate a several extra nodes and edges, whch should be filtered out at the last step, by solving an ILP with constraints.

Now in the last step, the authors attach an indicator variable $y$ to each detection and to each edge. They then state the cost of choosing a cell/nucleus is set equivalent to the (negative of the) cell/nucleus prediction at that pixel/voxel from their trained network. Similarly the cost of selecting an edge is set equal to the distance between the estimated nucleus position at the $t-1 th$ time step and the linked nucleus position at the $t-1 th$ time step. More specifically, the cost for choosing a nucleus (node) is

$$C_{i}^{V} = \tau^{V} + w^{V} s_{i}$$

and the cost of choosing an edge is :

$$c^{E}_{i} = w^{E} d_{i}$$

The constants ($\tau^{V}, w^{E}, w^{V}, ...$) are obtained by grid search while trying to minimize the total number of errors on the validation subset. (This is an interesting idea as well since generally we try to optimize the TRA score on the validation subset but I have noticed that the TRA score is not expressive enough at times). The constraints that are put in place are: (CONSISTENCY) if an edge is chosen then the vertices should also be chosen, (CONTINUATION) either an edge continues or the node is marked as the beginning or end of track and (SPLIT) the number of incoming edges to any node is <= 2. These are formally worded as follows:

For an edge $e = (v, u)$, the CONSISTENCY states that 

$$ 2 y_{e} <= y_{v} + y_{u}$$

For CONTINUATION

$$\sum_{p \in P_{v}} y_{p} + y_{v}^{A} - y_{v} = 0 $$

$$ \sum_{n \in N_{v}} -y_{n} - y_{v}^{D} + y_{v} <=0$$

For SPLIT
$$\sum_{n \in N_{v}} y_{n} + y_{v}^{D} - 2 y_{v} <=0$$

While quite elegant, a weakness of this formulation is that it can not correct for over segmentation mistakes (which `Kit-Sch-Ge` could) since that would flout the CONTINUATION constraint.

One additional cool thing about `Linajea` is their approach towards metrics. Instead of using the metrics commonly available (such as `TRA` etc), the authors first highlight five different tracking errors - false positive edges, false negative edges, identity switches, false positive divisions and false negative divisions. The authors reckon that with only GT point detections for each nucleus, false positive edges can not be determined (I guess that here the authors mean that without the instance masks, it is not straightforward to assign the predicted detections to the GT detections and then subsequently make a judgement about whether there is an edge or not. How is it straightforward to do the same for false negative edges?)

In their [github](https://github.com/funkelab/linajea/tree/master/linajea) code, they distinguish a minimal version which depends on `solver.py` and `track.py` and a non minimal version which depends on `non_minimal_solver.py` and `non_minimal_track.py`.

Now changing gears, in the classical work by [Jaqaman et al, 2008](https://www.nature.com/articles/nmeth.1237), the authors provide the pseudo-global solution to tracking by first linking detection candidates between frames in a temporally greedy manner and then re-iding or re-linking the track segments generated in the first step to close gaps and handle splitting or merging events, by a temporally global optimization. 

>"Here we present a tracking algorithm that uses one mathematical framework, the linear assignment problem (LAP), to provide an accurate solution to all the SPT challenges listed above. Given a set of detected particles throughout a time-lapse image sequence, the algorithm first links the detected particles between consecutive frames, and then links the track segments generated in the first step to simultaneously close gaps and capture particle merge and split events. Thus, although the initial particle assignment is temporally greedy, the subsequent track segment assignment is accomplished via temporally global optimization, overcoming the shortcomings of algorithms relying solely on greedy assignment strategies. Both steps use global optimization in space. Overall, this approach defines an accurate, yet computationally feasible, approximation to MHT, allowing the robust tracking of particles under high-density conditions."


In contrast to the first step of Linaea where a candidate graph is constructed which tends to be somewhat dense and needs to be sparsified, in Jaqaman et al, the first step of linking candidates between frames is performed with the constraint that each detection can be mapped to at most one detection at the timepoint before and after - which leads to a sparse graph across time, since many correct detections may not be linked due too under segmentation, cell division events are not captured because of te constraint etc


>"The track segments obtained in this step tended to be incomplete, resulting in a systematic underestimation of particle lifetimes. In addition, because of the one-to-one assignment of particles, this step could not capture particle merges and splits, which by definition required one particle in one frame to be assigned to two particles in the previous or subsequent frame, respectively. Therefore, in a second step, we linked the initial track segments in three ways: (i) end to start, to close gaps resulting from temporary disappearance, (ii) end to middle, to capture merging events, and (iii) start to middle, to capture splitting events."

The cost for gap closing $\delta_{IJ}$ is defined to be the square of the euclidean distance between the end of track segment $I$ and start of track segment $J$
The cost for splitting events is defined as:

$$
s_{IJ} = \delta_{IJ}^{2} \times \rho_{IJ}, \text{ if } \rho_{IJ} > 1
s_{IJ} = \delta_{IJ}^{2} \times \rho_{IJ}^{-2}, \text{ if } \rho_{IJ} < 1
$$

Here,
$$
\rho_{IJ} = \frac{A_{J}(t-1)}{A_{I} (t) + A_{J} (t)}
$$

The assumption is that after a splitting or cell division event, the intensity of the track segment is different and should be factored alongside the euclidean distances of tracklets being compared. 

This is a neat approach to perform linking of track segments, rather than individual nodes and could definitely be included as an alternative approach for EmbedTrack. I especially like that for splitting events, they consider each (track segment, time frame) tuple.
