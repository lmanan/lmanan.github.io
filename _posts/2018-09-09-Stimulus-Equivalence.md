(From [Pollen, Lee and Taylor (1971)](http://science.sciencemag.org/content/sci/173/3991/74.full.pdf))
> "Thirty years ago, Lashley considered the problem of how we see an object as the same object regardless of its position and apparent size. He considered the problem of "stimulus equivalence" as the most elementary problem of cerebral organization and doubted that any progress toward a genuine understanding of nervous integration would be achieved until this problem was solved." 

Reading up on Hubel and Wiesel 's experiments recently got me thinking about how we manage to quickly process the spatio temporal world around us. [Daugman (1985)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/4020513) neatly summarized the two possible theories: when we look at an object, do we internally generate its characteristic features (covariant point descriptors)? Or, is it that the visual cortical cells do a Fourier decomposition of the visual field into contributions from various spatial frequencies? Or, are these two theories actually equivalent and that the subject of their equivalence is similar to the long-standing but illusory particle-wave dichotomy? (Daugman seemed to think so!)

I further wonder how the process of object recognition occurs, say when we chance to look upon the same object again. If the process of encoding the visual field as a set of point descriptors is akin to compression, would the act of object recognition, implemented by accessing and matching across a database of such sets, have elements of *decompression*?

This reminds me of the opening tale from [The Man Who Mistook His Wife for a Hat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Man_Who_Mistook_His_Wife_for_a_Hat), where the patient when asked to describe a scene, could identify its features but was unable to make sense of the scene as a whole. Going by the ideas suggested above, would it be right to say then that his (visual) feature-encoding processes were functioning normally, but the *decompression* or decoding was somehow defective?! Here is an extract:

> "He saw all right, but what did he see? I opened out a copy of the National Geographic Magazine and asked him to describe some pictures in it. His responses here were very curious. His eyes would dart from one thing to another, picking up tiny features, individual features, as they had done with my face. A striking brightness, a color, a shape would arrest his attention and elicit comment - but in no case did he get the scene as a whole. He failed to see the whole, seeing only details, which he spotted like blips on a radar screen. He never entered into relation with the picture as a whole, never faced, so to speak, its physiognomy. He had no sense of a landscape or scene."
